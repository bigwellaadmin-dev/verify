# Appendix: Research Foundation

*Teaching AI to Ask · The VERIFY Framework · Teacher Guide · Sandra Robinson · Big Wella*

The footnotes throughout this guide point to specific claims and their sources. This appendix gives a broader account of the research that shaped the framework: what the studies found, why it matters for classroom practice, and where the limits of the evidence are.

The summaries here are plain language. They are not substitutes for the original sources, which are listed at the end of this section for readers who want to go deeper.

## Cognitive offloading and the Law of Less Work

The starting point for VERIFY is a well-established principle in cognitive science: people systematically choose the path that requires the least cognitive effort when multiple options are available. This is not a character flaw. It is how human cognition manages limited working memory under conditions of uncertainty and time pressure.

In an AI-assisted learning environment, the path of least cognitive effort is to accept the AI output without interrogation. The output is polished, structured, and immediately available. The alternative, thinking carefully about the task, examining the source, reflecting on fit, investigating gaps, filtering for quality, is harder and slower. Without explicit instruction and teacher presence, most students will take the easier path.

The research term for this pattern is cognitive offloading: the outsourcing of cognitive work to an external system. Cognitive offloading is not new. Students have offloaded to calculators, spell checkers, and the internet for decades. What is different about AI is the completeness of the offload. A calculator performs one operation and returns a number the student still has to interpret. An AI tool performs the reasoning, the structuring, the argument construction, and the writing, and returns a finished-seeming product. The student has nothing left to do except accept or reject it.

The research on what this costs is consistent. Students who offload cognitive work produce better-looking short-term outputs and worse long-term understanding. They do not build the mental structures that would allow them to apply the knowledge in a new context. The output is there. The learning is not.

> **The Corvinus finding**
>
> A 2024 experiment at Corvinus University of Budapest found that students given unrestricted AI access produced polished work but scored near-zero on a subsequent offline knowledge test. An analysis of their submissions found a median of 100% AI-generated content. The experiment was revised before completion following student backlash, but salvaged data provided the clearest available evidence of the gap between AI-assisted output quality and actual knowledge retention.

## Productive struggle

The research on learning is consistent on one point: the cognitive effort of grappling with difficult material is the effort that produces durable understanding. This is not an argument for making things unnecessarily hard. It is an argument for the specific value of the difficulty that comes from engaging seriously with a complex task.

Psychologists call this productive struggle: the effortful cognitive work involved in drafting and revising, interrogating a source and finding it wanting, forming a position and testing it against counterargument. Students who do this work develop the mental structures that allow them to transfer knowledge to new contexts. Students who skip it produce output without understanding.

The specific challenge AI creates for productive struggle is that the output removes the visible signals that normally trigger it. A rough first draft prompts revision because the roughness is visible. An AI output has no visible roughness. It looks finished. The student who accepts it has no signal that the productive struggle has not happened.

VERIFY is designed to create that signal artificially. The check questions at F, the reflection at R, the verbal defence at Y, are all structured interventions that make the absence of productive struggle visible to the student before the work is submitted. They do not guarantee productive struggle happens. They make it much harder to avoid without noticing.

> **What the research says about making students struggle**
>
> You cannot compel productive struggle. The research is clear on this. What you can do is design conditions where avoiding the struggle costs more than doing it. Visible process assessment, named discomfort, and tasks with genuine stakes are the three conditions that reliably shift the calculation.

## Meta-Task Awareness

Meta-Task Awareness is the capacity to understand not just what a task asks you to do on the surface, but why it asks it and what thinking it is designed to produce. A student with high Meta-Task Awareness reads a task and asks: what am I being asked to demonstrate, and what would a strong response look like before I start? A student with low Meta-Task Awareness reads the same task and starts producing output.

The concept was developed in response to observed patterns in AI-assisted student work: students who produced technically competent responses that completely missed what the task was actually testing. The researchers concluded that the problem was not AI use per se, but the absence of a mental model for what the task required. Students who did not understand what the task was testing could not use AI to serve that purpose. They used it to produce something that looked like a response.

VERIFY's V step is Meta-Task Awareness made practical. The three questions, what am I being asked to demonstrate, who is this for, what kind of thinking does this require, operationalise MTA as a classroom habit. Students who answer these questions before prompting have done the metacognitive work that makes every subsequent step more productive.

## The illusion of mastery

One of the most consistent findings in the research on AI-assisted learning is the confidence gap: students who use AI assistance report higher confidence in their understanding than students who complete the same task without AI, while performing worse on subsequent assessments of that understanding.

The mechanism is straightforward. The quality of the AI output creates a feeling of completion. The student reads a polished, well-structured response and associates that quality with their own understanding. They did not produce the quality. They received it. But the feeling is the same.

This is the illusion of mastery: the sense of having understood something that was produced by an external system rather than built through one's own cognitive effort. It is not dishonesty. Students who report high confidence in AI-assisted tasks are not lying. They genuinely feel confident. The feeling is just not tracking the right thing.

VERIFY's R and Y steps are the primary interventions against the illusion of mastery. R asks students to pause and honestly assess whether they have done any cognitive work yet. Y asks whether they can defend the judgement in the work independently. Both steps puncture the illusion by requiring the student to produce evidence of their own thinking rather than relying on the quality of the output as a proxy.

## Equity and access in Australia

The research on AI in Australian education documents significant and consistent access gaps between students in different contexts. These gaps are not marginal. They shape which students can engage with AI tools at all, how reliably they can access them, and what quality of interaction is possible given the infrastructure available to them.

Research from the Australian Public Policy Institute documents that 76% of students in surveyed rural communities rely on mobile-only internet access, compared to 10.5% nationally. Disadvantaged schools report inadequate digital resources at nearly twice the rate of advantaged schools. Indigenous and rural students face the highest barriers to AI-enabled learning across all measured dimensions.

The implication for VERIFY is practical. A framework that assumes reliable broadband access, consistent device availability, and equal quality of AI interaction is a framework that will serve some students and not others. VERIFY is designed to work with whatever AI access a student has, because the habits it builds are cognitive rather than platform-specific. The framework teaches students to think about what they are doing with AI. That skill transfers across tools, across connection quality, and across whatever the technology landscape looks like in five years.

The perspective gap is a related but distinct issue. AI systems are trained on large bodies of text that reflect the contexts of their sources. For Australian students this means working with systems that skew toward northern hemisphere contexts, urban settings, and particular cultural and professional assumptions. Output on historical events, social issues, and cultural practices will centre some perspectives and marginalise others. VERIFY's I and F steps are where students learn to identify and respond to that gap.

> **Why this matters for teachers in well-resourced schools**
>
> The equity dimension is not only relevant to teachers working in under-resourced contexts. Students in well-resourced schools are also working with AI systems that do not know their specific local context, their community, or the particular circumstances of the task they have been set. The investigation habit built at I benefits every student regardless of their access level.

## The Chain of Learning

The Chain of Learning is a framework for understanding AI interaction as cumulative rather than transactional. Each prompt a student sends to an AI is not an isolated event. It is part of a sequence. The direction set at the first prompt shapes every subsequent exchange. A student who starts with a vague prompt and tries to fix the problem downstream is working against the chain, not with it.

The framework has a direct implication for classroom practice: the most important AI interaction in any task is the first one. The time invested in V, in constructing a precise prompt specification before the AI is opened, is an investment that pays at every subsequent step. A student who starts the chain well produces better output with less effort. A student who starts it poorly spends more time producing worse output.

The Chain of Learning also supports the case for I. Investigating further is not starting again. It is extending the chain with better material. A student who has established a clear direction at V and has examined and reflected on the initial output at E and R arrives at I knowing what they need. The investigation is targeted rather than general. It adds to the chain rather than replacing it.

## Where the evidence is limited

The research base for AI in education is moving fast and is not yet settled. Several claims in this guide are better supported than others, and teachers reading this deserve an honest account of where the limits are.

The cognitive science is solid. The Law of Less Work, productive struggle, metacognition, and the illusion of mastery are well-established across decades of research that predates AI. VERIFY's foundation in these principles is durable.

The AI-specific research is newer and in some cases based on small samples or single studies. The Corvinus experiment is compelling but was a single study that was modified before completion. The equity access data is from surveyed communities rather than nationally representative samples. The Meta-Task Awareness construct is recent and its measurement is still being developed.

The practitioner moves throughout this guide are applications derived from research principles rather than directly observed classroom interventions. They are informed by the evidence. They are not validated by it in the way that long-established teaching practices are.

This does not mean the framework is speculative. It means the teacher using it is part of the evidence base. Every time you run a V check and observe what students can and cannot do, every time a verbal defence at Y reveals whether learning happened, you are generating the kind of classroom evidence that the research is still catching up with. VERIFY is built on solid foundations. The specific moves are where the teaching practice and the research will continue to develop together.

> **The honest summary**
>
> The research says students will take the easy path if you let them, that the effort you prevent them from taking is the effort that produces learning, and that a present teacher asking specific questions is the most reliable mechanism for making that effort happen. Everything else in this guide is an elaboration of those three points.
