# Part 4: Assessment and Accountability

*Teaching AI to Ask · The VERIFY Framework · Teacher Guide · Sandra Robinson · Big Wella*

Assessment in the context of VERIFY is about making thinking visible. The product a student submits is evidence. The process they followed to get there is evidence. The verbal defence, the process reflection, the prompt specification, the investigation log — all of it is evidence. Part 4 covers how to design tasks that make that evidence collectable, and how to make judgements about it consistently.

## 4.1  Assessing process, not just product

Most school assessment is designed around products: the essay, the report, the presentation. Products are the easiest thing to collect and the hardest thing to attribute confidently in an AI-assisted environment. A polished product no longer tells a teacher much about whether the student who submitted it did the thinking the task was designed to produce.

VERIFY addresses this by making the process assessable. The prompt specification, the source reflection, the filter log, the ownership statement, and the verbal defence are all process artefacts. They are not additional tasks. They are structured evidence of the thinking that should have produced the final product. A student who produces a strong product and cannot produce any of the process artefacts has told the teacher something important.

### What process assessment looks like in practice

Process assessment does not require a new assessment system. It requires adding two or three process artefacts to what you already collect. The most useful combinations are:

Prompt specification + process reflection: collected with the final product. Takes five minutes to write, five minutes to read. Reveals the V and R steps immediately.

Source reflection + filter log: collected at submission. Shows whether E and F happened. A student whose filter log says I changed nothing and whose source reflection names no specific sources has told you where the gaps are.

Verbal defence: three to five minutes per student, assessable or not. The most direct evidence available for Y. Can be run as a class conversation rather than individual interviews if time is the constraint.

> **On workload**
>
> Process assessment does not have to mean more marking. A prompt specification takes thirty seconds to read. A process reflection takes two minutes. A verbal defence takes five. The information yield per minute is higher than marking a finished essay, because the process artefacts tell you where the thinking broke down rather than just how polished the product is.

## 4.2  Task design for visible thinking

The most effective VERIFY tasks are designed so that the process is embedded rather than added on. The following design principles produce tasks where the thinking is visible by construction.

### Build the V specification into the task brief

Include the three V questions in the task brief itself. Ask students to submit their prompt specification alongside their work. This normalises the V step as part of the task rather than as an extra requirement, and gives the teacher a reference point for assessing whether the final work actually addressed what the student said they were trying to do.

### Require at least one locally sourced or personally verified claim

Design tasks that cannot be completed entirely with generic AI output. Require one source that the student has specifically verified, or one piece of information that comes from their own context rather than from a training dataset. This forces I to happen and makes E necessary. A student who cannot produce a locally sourced or verified claim at submission has not done the investigation the task required.

### Make the counterargument visible

Include in the task brief a requirement to acknowledge one opposing view or limitation. This operationalises F question 3 and makes fairness assessable rather than abstract. A student who has applied F honestly can name the counterargument and explain why they have maintained their position despite it.

### Assess the defence separately from the product

When the verbal defence is a separate assessment component, even weighted at 10 or 15 percent, students prepare for it differently. They track their reasoning as they work because they know they will need to reproduce it. The preparation for the defence produces better VERIFY engagement across the whole task.

> **The design test**
>
> Before setting a task, ask whether a student could complete it satisfactorily using only AI output without engaging with any step of VERIFY. If the answer is yes, the task needs redesigning. A task that rewards genuine thinking over produced output is a VERIFY-aligned task.

## 4.3  Rubrics

Two versions of the VERIFY rubric follow. The first uses A to E language aligned to the QCAA standards elaboration format, where C represents the achievement standard.[^1] The second uses a four-level format for teachers working outside Queensland or in contexts that use different reporting language.

Both rubrics assess the VERIFY process, not the quality of the final product. They are designed to be used alongside subject-specific rubrics that assess content knowledge and skill, not instead of them. VERIFY is a process framework. The subject rubric assesses what the student produced. The VERIFY rubric assesses how they produced it.

> **How to use these rubrics**
>
> Select the criteria most relevant to your task. You do not need to assess all six letters in every task. Assess the letters you have explicitly taught and checked. Weight the criteria according to what your task emphasises. V and Y tend to carry most weight because they bookend the process. E and F carry more weight in research-heavy tasks. R and I carry more weight in tasks that require specific contextual knowledge.

## 4.4  Feedback language for each letter

The following phrases are starting points for written and verbal feedback aligned to each VERIFY step. They are not scripts. Use them as a base and adapt to the specific student and task.[^2]

### V

- **Strong:** Your prompt specification was precise. You named the thinking the task required before you started, and the output reflects that.
- **Developing:** Your specification named the topic but not the thinking type or the audience. Next task, spend two more minutes on V before you open the AI.
- **Weak:** There is no evidence that you validated the task before prompting. The output you received reflects a generic interpretation of the topic, not the specific task you were set.

### E

- **Strong:** You identified a claim that could not be verified and went to find a better source. That is exactly what E asks for.
- **Developing:** You checked one claim but accepted the rest without question. At least two of the claims in your work cannot be sourced. Next task, apply the source check to three claims, not one.
- **Weak:** The work contains claims that are not accurate. There is no evidence that you interrogated the AI output before using it.

### R

- **Strong:** You identified that the output was written for a generic audience and adjusted it specifically for your context. That adjustment is visible in the final work and it is better for it.
- **Developing:** Your reflection log says you thought the output was a good fit, but the final work does not address the specific requirements of the task. The R step needs more time next task.
- **Weak:** There is no evidence that you asked whether the output was relevant to your specific task and context. The work reads as if it was written for someone else.

### I

- **Strong:** You identified that the AI output had no Australian data and went and found it. The specific source you added made your argument more defensible and more relevant.
- **Developing:** You added one source but it largely repeated what the AI had already provided. Next task, look for something the AI could not have given you: local data, lived experience, a specific context.
- **Weak:** The work contains nothing beyond what the AI provided. There is no evidence that you asked what might be missing.

### F

- **Strong:** Your filter log shows three specific changes you made as a result of the check questions, including adding the counterargument. The work is stronger for each of them.
- **Developing:** Your filter log says you changed nothing. Look at check question 5 specifically: who is missing from this answer? There is at least one perspective that is absent from the work.
- **Weak:** There is no evidence of filtering. The work accepts the AI output's framing without question, including a claim that is not well supported.

### Y

- **Strong:** You could explain your reasoning fluently and specifically when I asked. The argument in the work is yours and you can defend it. That is the goal.
- **Developing:** You could summarise the work but struggled to explain why you chose one argument over another. Before the next task, track your reasoning as you go so you can account for it at Y.
- **Weak:** When I asked you to explain your reasoning, you described the output rather than your own thinking. The judgement in this work does not appear to be yours.

## 4.5  A note on academic integrity

VERIFY is not an academic integrity tool and should not be framed as one.[^3] Its purpose is to produce genuine thinking, not to detect the absence of it. A student who has worked through VERIFY honestly is demonstrating integrity through the process, not because they are being monitored.

That said, the process artefacts VERIFY generates — the prompt specification, the source reflection, the filter log, the verbal defence — are among the most reliable indicators of genuine engagement currently available to teachers. A student who can produce all of them is very likely to have done the cognitive work the task required. A student who cannot produce them has told the teacher something important.

Use this as formative information, not punitive. The student who arrives at Y with nothing to defend has identified their own gap. The teacher's job is to respond to that information productively, not to pursue an integrity case on the basis of it.

> **The framing that works**
>
> Tell students: VERIFY is not about catching you out. It is about making sure you got something out of this task. The verbal defence is not a test of whether you used AI. It is a test of whether you learned anything. Those are different questions, and only one of them matters here.
