# Part 3: Using VERIFY in Practice

*Teaching AI to Ask · The VERIFY Framework · Teacher Guide · Sandra Robinson · Big Wella*

Part 2 explained what each step of VERIFY is and why it matters. Part 3 shows what it looks like to use the framework across a full task sequence. The first three sections cover the teacher moves before, during, and after any task. The final section gives worked examples in four subject areas.

The moves in this section are not scripts. They are patterns that have emerged from the research on productive struggle, metacognitive teaching, and the specific challenge of AI-assisted learning. Use them as starting points and adapt them to your students and context.

## 3.1  Before the task

The work teachers do before students open the AI is the most important work in the framework. What happens at V determines the quality of everything that follows. The moves below are sequenced: do them in order the first time you introduce VERIFY. Once the framework is embedded, the sequence becomes faster and eventually part of how you set up any task that involves AI.

### Introduce the task as a thinking problem, not a production problem

Most students approach tasks as production problems: something needs to be produced, the task is done when it is produced. Before students start, reframe the task explicitly. Tell them what kind of thinking the task is designed to produce and what you will be looking for as evidence of that thinking. Not what the finished piece looks like. What the thinking underneath it looks like.

This reframe is not subtle and it does not need to be. You can say it directly: the goal of this task is not the document you submit. The goal is the thinking you do to get there. The document is how you show me the thinking happened.

> **The most important before-task move**
>
> Name Y explicitly. Tell students that the final question for this task is whether they can explain what they decided and why, in their own words, without looking at their work. Not as a threat. As the definition of what the task is trying to produce.

### Build the V questions before the AI opens

Give students the task and ask them to answer three questions in writing before they touch the AI.

What am I being asked to demonstrate? Not the topic. The thinking the teacher is looking for.

Who is this for? The audience shapes every decision that follows.

What kind of thinking does this require? Analysis, argument, synthesis, evaluation. Naming it makes it harder to outsource.

From those answers, students write a prompt specification. Not a question. A specification that names the task, the audience, the thinking required, and any constraints. The first time you do this, model it. Show the class what a vague prompt returns versus a specified one. The contrast does the teaching.

### Name what E, R, and F will ask

Before the task starts, tell students what checkpoints are coming. At some point during this task I will ask you to tell me the source for a claim. At some point I will ask you what you actually think about this topic. At some point I will ask you to tell me whose perspective is missing from your work.

Naming the checkpoints in advance changes how students work. A student who knows the source question is coming pays more attention to sources while working. A student who knows the perspective question is coming notices perspective gaps as they read. The checkpoints do not have to be surprises to be effective. Knowing they are coming is what makes them effective.

## 3.2  During the task

The during-task moves are where the teacher presence that makes VERIFY work becomes visible. These are not interruptions to the learning. They are the learning. A teacher who circulates, asks specific questions, and responds to what they find is doing the most important work in the framework.

### The V check

Early in the task, before students have committed to a direction, ask a student to read you their prompt specification. Not the AI output. The specification they wrote before prompting. If they do not have one, that is the V check result. Send them back to write it before they continue.

If they have one, ask whether the output they have received actually addresses it. This is the moment where V connects to E: the student compares what they asked for against what they got. Many students will find a gap here they had not noticed.

### The E check

Mid-task, ask a student to tell you the source for one specific claim in their current output. Pick a claim that looks confident. The student who has examined the source can tell you immediately. The student who has not will look at the claim as if for the first time.

The follow-on question is always the same: how would you find out whether that claim is right? Not can you check it right now. How would you go about checking it. The answer tells the teacher what the student's relationship to evidence is.

### The R check

When the work looks like it might be finishing, ask the student what they think about the topic. Not what the work says. What they think. Two sentences is enough to reveal whether R has happened.

If the student gives back the output as their opinion, ask whether they agree with it. If they say yes, ask why. If they cannot say why, they have identified their own R gap without the teacher having to name it.

### The I check

Ask: what does this output not know about your specific situation? If the student cannot identify anything, ask whose experience this is written from and whether that is the same as theirs. One of those questions will open the investigation.

For students who are stuck or who have produced something generic, I is the entry point. The question what is missing from this answer does not require any prior work to be useful. Start there and follow the thread wherever it leads.

### The F check

Pick one check question from the F list and ask the student to apply it to their current work, out loud, in front of you. Not all seven. One. The answer tells the teacher more than a submitted piece of work ever will.

Question 1 and question 5 are the most productive during-task checks. Source for a claim, and who is missing from this answer. Both produce immediate, specific, actionable responses.

> **On pacing**
>
> You will not run all six checks on every student in every task. That is not the goal. The goal is to run enough checks that students know the checks are coming, which changes how they work from the start. Two or three targeted checks per student per task, consistently applied, produces more learning than a comprehensive check that happens once.[^2]

## 3.3  After the task

The after-task moves close the learning loop. They make the process visible to both the student and the teacher, create the evidence base for assessment, and build the habits that make VERIFY faster in the next task.

### The process reflection

Ask students to write a short process reflection before they submit. Three questions, one sentence each.

What did you specify in your initial prompt, and what would you change about that now? Connects V to the finished work and makes the prompt construction visible as a decision.

What in this piece is yours, and what did you take from AI and adapt? The ownership question. Makes the boundary visible without requiring a confession.

What did you change as a result of the F check questions, and why? Makes the filtering visible. A student who changed nothing writes that and says why they were satisfied.

These reflections are not primarily for the teacher. They are for the student. The act of writing them makes the process conscious in a way that changes how the student approaches the next task. The teacher benefits from reading them, but the learning happens in the writing.

### The verbal defence

For tasks where the thinking is the main thing being assessed, a brief verbal defence is the most direct evidence available. Three to five minutes. Two or three questions from the teacher. The student answers from their own thinking without looking at their work.

This does not have to be formally assessed. Its primary function is as feedback. The student who struggles to defend their work at Y gets immediate, specific information about where the gap is. So does the teacher.

### Feeding forward

The most useful thing a teacher can do after a task is name one specific thing each student did well in the VERIFY cycle and one specific thing to work on in the next task. Not general feedback on the product. Specific feedback on the process.

Tell the student: your V specification was strong and produced specific output. Your R check was surface level. Next task, I want you to spend more time on R before you decide you are finished. That feedback is more useful than any mark because it tells the student exactly where to put their effort.

> **The accumulation principle**
>
> VERIFY builds over multiple tasks. The first task will be slow and incomplete. The fifth will be faster. The tenth will have students running parts of the cycle automatically before the teacher prompts them. The goal is not perfection on the first task. The goal is a gradually internalising habit.

## 3.4  Worked examples

The following examples show VERIFY applied in four subject areas. English and Science show the full cycle. History and Technology / Design show a single teacher intervention at the moment it matters most.

The examples below are constructed from real classroom patterns. They show what those patterns look like when they work. Your version will be messier, slower, and occasionally go nowhere. That is not a failure of the framework. It is teaching. The moves are still the right moves.

The students, tasks, and conversations are constructed from the patterns described in the research and in the practitioner moves above. They are illustrative, not transcripts.[^1]

### English — Full example

Year 10 English. Task: write a persuasive essay arguing for or against a position on social media and adolescent mental health. The essay must use at least two sources and present a clear, evidenced argument.

#### V: Before the task

The teacher gives the class five minutes to answer the three V questions before opening the AI. One student, Priya, writes:

> **Priya's V**
>
> What am I being asked to demonstrate? A clear argument with evidence. Who is this for? The teacher, but also someone who might disagree with my position. What kind of thinking? Argument and evaluation. I need to pick a side and defend it, not just describe both.

From this, Priya writes a prompt specification: Write a persuasive essay arguing that social media has a net negative effect on adolescent mental health. The audience is an educated adult who is sceptical of this position. Use evidence from research. Year 10 level. 800 words.

The teacher circulates and checks prompt specifications before anyone opens the AI. Another student, Marcus, has written: social media mental health essay. The teacher stops and asks him the three V questions. He cannot answer the second one. They spend two minutes on audience before he opens the AI. His output is noticeably more specific than his previous tasks.

#### E: Examining the output

Priya receives a well-structured essay. The teacher asks her to identify the three most important claims and tell her where each one comes from. Priya can name two. The third, a statistic about depression rates, has no source she can identify. The teacher asks: how would you find out whether that number is right? Priya searches and finds a different figure from a different study. She notes the discrepancy and decides to use the verified figure with a citation, dropping the AI's version.

The teacher also asks Priya whose perspective is centred in the essay. Priya reads it back and realises all the research cited is American. She notes this as something to investigate at I.

#### R: The reflection check

Mid-task, the teacher asks Priya what she actually thinks about social media and mental health, separate from what she is arguing. Priya says she uses social media constantly and does not think it has hurt her, but she can see how it could. The teacher asks whether that complexity is in her essay. Priya looks at the draft and says no, it is all one-sided. The teacher asks whether that is a problem for a persuasive essay. Priya thinks about it and says no, but she wants to add a line that acknowledges the other side is not without evidence. She does.

#### I: Investigating further

Based on what she found at E, Priya looks for Australian research on social media and adolescent mental health. She finds a 2023 study from the Australian Institute of Family Studies. The findings are more mixed than the US research. She adjusts her argument to acknowledge the Australian context and uses the local study as her second source. Her essay is now more specific and more defensible than the AI draft.

#### F: The filter check

Priya applies question 3: what would someone who disagreed with this say, and does the essay mention it? She adds a paragraph acknowledging that correlation is not causation and that some research shows social media can have positive effects for isolated young people. Her argument is stronger for including it.

She applies question 6: is this written for my specific situation? She decides it is now, given she has the Australian source. She makes a note in her process reflection.

#### Y: The defence

The teacher asks Priya to talk her through the essay's argument in two minutes without looking at it. Priya does this without difficulty. She can explain why she chose her sources, why she included the counterargument, and why she thinks the position is defensible even given the mixed evidence. The teacher asks one challenge question: what would you say to someone who argued the Australian research undermines your position? Priya answers directly. The thinking is hers.

> **What this example shows**
>
> Priya's final essay is better than the AI draft in every measurable way: more specific sources, an Australian context, a genuine acknowledgement of counterargument, and an argument the student can defend verbally. None of those improvements required the teacher to mark a draft. They required the teacher to ask specific questions at specific moments.

### Science — Full example

Year 9 Science. Task: research a current environmental issue and explain the scientific evidence behind it, the human causes, and two possible responses. The report must demonstrate understanding of the science, not just describe it.

#### V: Before the task

The teacher asks the class to write their V specification before opening the AI. Student Jordan writes: Write a science report on ocean acidification for a Year 9 student who knows basic chemistry. Explain the science clearly, include human causes, and give two specific responses with evidence for why they might work. The teacher reads this and asks Jordan one question: what does demonstrate understanding of the science mean to you, specifically? Jordan thinks and adds: I need to explain the chemical process, not just name it.

The teacher notes this addition. It is the kind of precision that makes the V step matter. Jordan's prompt now asks for explanation rather than description.

#### E: Examining the output

Jordan's AI output explains ocean acidification clearly at a surface level. The teacher asks Jordan to identify where the chemistry explanation is. Jordan finds two sentences that name the process but do not explain the mechanism. The teacher asks: could you explain that mechanism to me right now, in your own words, without looking at this? Jordan cannot.

This is the E moment. The output looks like it contains the science. Jordan has not processed the science. The teacher directs Jordan to a specific section of their class notes and asks them to come back when they can explain the carbonate system in their own words. Jordan returns fifteen minutes later. The explanation in the final report is Jordan's, written after understanding, not transcribed from the AI.

#### R: The reflection check

The teacher asks Jordan whether ocean acidification affects Australian waters specifically. Jordan does not know. The teacher asks whether that matters for the task. Jordan decides it does and notes it as something to investigate.

#### I: Investigating further

Jordan searches for Australian ocean acidification data and finds information from the Australian Institute of Marine Science. The data on the Great Barrier Reef is more specific and more alarming than the generic global data the AI provided. Jordan incorporates this into the report's human causes section, connecting the global mechanism to a specific Australian context. The report is now doing what the task asked: demonstrating understanding, not just describing it.

#### F: The filter check

The teacher asks Jordan to apply question 1: can you point to a source for the three most important claims? Jordan has two sourced and one that came from the AI output without verification. They check it and find it is accurate but decide to source it anyway. The report now has three cited claims.

The teacher also asks question 2: does this answer the question I actually asked, or an easier version of it? Jordan reads the task again and realises the two responses section is thin. They add a paragraph on one response that explains specifically why the evidence supports it rather than just naming it.

#### Y: The defence

The teacher asks Jordan to explain the carbonate chemistry to her in two minutes. Jordan does this fluently. The teacher asks what the two responses are and which Jordan thinks is more likely to work. Jordan has a view and can explain it. The work is Jordan's.

> **What this example shows**
>
> The key moment in this example is the E check where the teacher asks Jordan to explain the science without looking at the output. That question revealed that the output contained the right words but Jordan had not processed them. Everything that followed from that moment produced genuine understanding rather than transcription.

### History — Single intervention

Year 8 History. Task: explain the causes of World War One and evaluate which cause was most significant. The student must make and defend a judgement.

#### The moment: R and Y

Student Aiden has produced a well-structured response that lists four causes and concludes that the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand was the most significant. The teacher asks Aiden to explain why he chose that cause over the alliance system, which was also in his response.

Aiden says: because the assassination started it.

The teacher asks: if the assassination had not happened, do you think the war still would have started eventually?

Aiden pauses. He says: maybe. Because of the alliances.

The teacher asks: so which is more significant, the trigger or the conditions that made the trigger matter?

Aiden rewrites his conclusion. He argues that the alliance system was the more significant cause because it transformed a local conflict into a world war. The assassination was the trigger. The alliances were the mechanism. His judgement is now his own and he can defend it.

> **What this intervention shows**
>
> The teacher asked two questions. Neither required knowledge the teacher did not already have. Both required Aiden to think rather than transcribe. The rewritten conclusion is better history and it is Aiden's thinking, not the AI's.

### Technology / Design — Single intervention

Year 9 Technology. Task: research a design problem, identify user needs, and propose a design solution with justification. The justification must connect the design decisions to the user needs.

#### The moment: V and I

Student Mei has used AI to generate a design proposal for a community garden app. The proposal is polished. The teacher asks Mei who the user is.

Mei says: people who want to grow vegetables.

The teacher asks: which people? Where? What do they currently have access to? What would make this app useful to them specifically rather than to anyone?

Mei has not thought about this. The AI proposal is generic. The teacher asks: is there a community near your school that actually has a community garden? Mei thinks and says yes, there is one two streets away. The teacher asks: have you talked to anyone who uses it?

Mei has not. The teacher suggests she spends fifteen minutes at the next lesson talking to the coordinator. Mei does. The redesigned proposal is specific to that community's actual needs: offline functionality because the garden has poor mobile reception, simple navigation because many users are elderly, a seasonal planting guide for the Brisbane climate.

The final proposal justifies every design decision with reference to a specific user need. The AI draft justified nothing. The difference is not the quality of the AI. It is the quality of the investigation.

> **What this intervention shows**
>
> The teacher asked Mei who the user is. That question exposed a V gap that ran through the entire proposal. The investigation that followed, including a real conversation with a real user, produced design thinking the AI could not have generated because it required local, specific, human knowledge.
