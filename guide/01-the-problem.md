# Part 1: The Problem With Easy Answers

*Sandra Robinson, Big Wella · Teaching AI to Ask · The VERIFY Framework · Teacher Guide*

## How to use this guide

This guide is for teachers who already know their presence in the classroom matters. It is not a framework students can run independently. It requires a teacher who leans in, who does check-ins, and who makes the content worth struggling with. That is not an accident of design. It is the point.

Read Part 1 before introducing VERIFY to students. It explains why the framework is built the way it is and what it is actually trying to do. Part 2 takes each letter in depth. Part 3 gives you the practical moves. Part 4 covers assessment.

You do not need to implement every section at once. Start with one letter. Use it consistently. Add the next when the first feels embedded.

> **The central argument**
>
> AI does not make students worse thinkers. The absence of explicit teaching makes students worse thinkers. VERIFY is the framework that puts the teaching back in.

## 1.1 What is actually happening

Watch a class of students open an AI tool for the first time. Most of them will copy the task they were given and paste it directly into the prompt field. They are not being careless. They are doing what is rational in the absence of any instruction about what prompting actually requires.

The AI returns something polished, structured, and confident. The student reads it, decides it looks right, and submits it or builds on it without interrogation. The task is done. The learning has not started.

This is not a behaviour problem. It is a knowledge gap. Students are missing a mental model for what a prompt is, how it differs from a question, and why the framing of a request shapes the quality of what comes back. Nobody taught them that. The tools arrived before the instruction did.

### The mechanism behind the behaviour

Cognitive scientists describe this pattern as the Law of Less Work: individuals systematically avoid cognitive effort when a path of lower resistance is available. This is not laziness. It is how human cognition operates under conditions of uncertainty and time pressure. When students do not know what good prompting looks like, the easiest available action is to copy what is already in front of them.

The consequence is what researchers call cognitive offloading: the outsourcing of thinking to an external system. The student has not stopped thinking. They have redirected their cognitive effort away from the task itself and toward the management of the AI output. The result looks like work. It is not work in the sense that produces learning.

### What cognitive offloading costs

The research is consistent on this point. AI can improve short-term performance on tasks while simultaneously harming long-term learning and knowledge transfer. Students who outsource the cognitive work of a task do not build the mental structures that would allow them to apply that knowledge in a new context. They have the output. They do not have the understanding.

> **Research note**
>
> A randomised experiment at Corvinus University of Budapest found that students given unrestricted AI access produced polished work but scored near-zero on a subsequent offline knowledge test. An analysis of final submissions found a median of 100% AI-generated content. The researchers concluded that AI tools had become indispensable for students in ways that raised fundamental questions about whether any learning had occurred.[^1]

Psychologist Howard Gardner describes the risk in terms of the disciplined, synthesising, and creative minds: the cognitive capacities that allow humans to do things AI cannot. When students consistently outsource the effort that builds those capacities, they do not develop them. The research describes this outcome as metacognitive laziness: a pattern of dependency on AI assistance that gradually erodes a student's ability to manage their own learning.[^2]

This is not a future risk. It is documented in current classrooms. The question for teachers is not whether it is happening. It is what to do about it.

> **The tradeoff to name honestly**
>
> Telling students not to use AI is not a viable position. The tools are available, the incentive to use them is strong, and prohibition does not produce the thinking skills students need. The question is how to teach students to use AI in ways that preserve rather than replace cognitive effort.

## 1.2 What AI cannot know about your students

AI systems produce fluent, confident output regardless of whether that output is right for the specific student, task, or context in front of them. The system does not know what your student is trying to demonstrate. It does not know what level of complexity is appropriate, what the teacher's actual learning intention is, or what the student already understands and what they still need to build.

It also does not know who is missing from its answers.

### The access gap

In Australia, students do not arrive at AI tools from equivalent positions. Research documents that 76% of students in surveyed rural communities rely on mobile-only internet access, compared to 10.5% nationally. Disadvantaged schools report inadequate digital resources at nearly twice the rate of advantaged schools.[^3]

These are not marginal differences. They shape which students can engage with AI tools at all, how reliably they can access them, and what quality of interaction is possible on a mobile connection versus a desktop with reliable broadband. A framework that assumes equal access is a framework that will serve some students and not others.

VERIFY is designed to work regardless of which tools a student can access, because the habits it builds are cognitive, not platform-specific. The framework teaches students to think about what they are doing with AI. That skill transfers across tools, across contexts, and across whatever the technology landscape looks like in five years.

### The perspective gap

AI systems are trained on large bodies of text. That text reflects the contexts, assumptions, and perspectives of its sources. For Australian students, this means working with systems that skew toward northern hemisphere contexts, urban settings, and particular cultural and professional assumptions that may not match their own experience or the content they are studying.

Students who do not know this will not question it. They will accept output that presents a partial perspective as a complete one. They will not notice whose voice is absent because they have not been taught to look for it.

This is not primarily a technical problem with AI systems. It is a teaching problem. Students need explicit instruction in the fact that AI output reflects a perspective, that perspectives have gaps, and that identifying those gaps is part of the work of using any source well.

> **The durable skill**
>
> The tools will change. The need to ask whose perspective is present and whose is missing will not. That question predates AI and will outlast whatever platform is dominant when your students graduate.

## 1.3 What this resource is asking of you

VERIFY will not work as a student self-management tool. It is not a checklist students complete before submitting work. It is a teaching framework that requires a teacher who is present, who checks in, and who has made a deliberate decision that the content is worth struggling with.

That is a significant ask. It is worth being direct about it.

### The teacher as the variable

The research on productive struggle is consistent: the cognitive effort required to grapple with uncertainty, to draft and revise, to interrogate a source and find it wanting, is the effort that produces durable learning. Students will not do that work voluntarily in the absence of a teacher who has made it matter.

What making it matter looks like in practice: a teacher who asks the right question at the right moment rather than accepting a finished-looking answer. A teacher who notices when a student has produced something polished and learned nothing. A teacher who treats the process of thinking as the assessable thing, not just the product.

This is not a new teaching skill. It is the teaching skill. VERIFY gives it a structure and a language.

### What VERIFY does not ask of you

It does not ask you to become a technology expert. It does not require specific platforms or tools. It does not require you to know more about AI than your students do.

It asks you to know what good thinking looks like in your subject area and to hold students to that standard even when AI makes it easier to accept something that merely looks like good thinking.

That is already what you do. VERIFY gives you a framework for doing it in an environment where the path of least resistance has become significantly easier to take.

### An honest note about what does not work

VERIFY will not work every time. It will not work with every student. There will be classes, days, and contexts where the conditions for productive struggle are not present and the framework produces compliance rather than thinking.

That is not a failure of the framework. It is the reality of teaching. The measure of VERIFY is not whether it produces thinking in every student every time. It is whether it gives teachers a coherent basis for creating conditions where genuine thinking is more likely to occur.

> **The position in one sentence**
>
> The goal is a student who has taken information, interacted with it, moulded it, and found ways to make it interesting to them rather than just completing a task. VERIFY is the framework that makes that goal teachable.
