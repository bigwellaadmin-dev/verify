# Part 2: The Framework

*Sandra Robinson, Big Wella · Teaching AI to Ask · The VERIFY Framework · Teacher Guide*

Part 2 takes each letter of VERIFY in depth. For each step you will find the mechanism underneath the behaviour, the classroom reality teachers recognise, and a set of practitioner moves informed by the research. The moves are not scripts. They are starting points that you will adapt to your subject, your students, and your context.

Each section can be read independently. If you are introducing one letter at a time, go directly to that section. If you are reading the framework as a whole, reading in order will show you how each step builds on and depends on the one before.

## V — Validate the Task

Before a student opens an AI tool, they need to know what they are actually trying to do. Not what the task sheet says. What the task is asking them to demonstrate, who the work is for, and what kind of thinking it requires. V is the step that makes that visible.

### The mechanism

Most students have never been taught what a prompt is. They have used search engines, which reward simple keyword queries. They have asked questions on social media, which reward brevity. When they arrive at an AI tool, they bring those habits with them.

The result is what researchers call a Meta-Task Awareness gap: the difference between understanding what a task asks you to do on the surface and understanding what kind of thinking it requires you to do underneath. A student with low Meta-Task Awareness reads a task, decides they understand it, and starts producing output. A student with high Meta-Task Awareness reads the same task and asks: what am I actually being asked to demonstrate here, and what would a strong response look like before I start?

The gap matters most at the AI interaction stage because AI returns something immediately. A search engine returns a list of links that require further evaluation. An AI tool returns a complete, structured, confident-sounding response. The student who has not validated their task before prompting accepts that response as an answer to a question they have not yet properly asked.

> **Research note**
>
> Meta-Task Awareness is identified in the research as a foundational literacy for AI-era learning: the capacity to understand not just what a task asks but why it asks it and what thinking it is designed to produce. Students who develop this capacity are better equipped to use AI as a thinking partner rather than a thinking replacement.[^1]

### Why prompting is a cognitive act

A prompt is not a question. It is a specification. It tells the AI what role to take, what the audience is, what level of complexity is appropriate, what constraints apply, and what the output needs to accomplish. A student who understands this can construct a prompt that returns something genuinely useful. A student who does not will get back something generic that happens to be about the right topic.

The act of constructing a good prompt requires the student to have already done some of the thinking the task is testing. They need to know what they are trying to demonstrate before they can specify it. This is not an obstacle to using AI. It is the point. V is the step that makes that thinking happen before the AI is opened, rather than being outsourced to it.

> **The tradeoff to name**
>
> Teaching prompt construction takes time at the front of a task that can feel like delay. It is not delay. A student who has validated their task before prompting will produce better work in less total time than a student who discovers the problem at the revision stage. V is an investment that pays at every subsequent step.

### The classroom reality

Teachers recognise the products of a missed V step immediately. The work covers the right topic but does not address the specific task. The argument is generic rather than contextualised. The student cannot explain what they were trying to show. When asked, they describe what the AI produced rather than what they intended.

A common variation: the student who produces something sophisticated and is genuinely surprised when it does not meet the brief. They put effort in. They interacted with the AI for a long time. They produced a long, well-structured piece of work. But they never stopped to ask whether the direction they were heading was the right one. The effort was real. The validation step was missing.

Another variation is harder to see: the student who interprets the task correctly but prompts so vaguely that the AI cannot return anything specific. The output is on-topic but shallow. The student reads it, senses something is wrong, and adds more prompts trying to fix it downstream. The problem was always at V.

### Practitioner moves

The following moves are practitioner applications informed by the research on Meta-Task Awareness, cognitive offloading, and productive struggle. They are starting points. Adapt them to your subject area and your students.

#### Before the task

The most effective V intervention happens before students open the AI. Give students the task and ask them to answer three questions in writing before they do anything else.

What am I being asked to demonstrate? Not what the topic is. What the teacher will be looking for as evidence of understanding.

Who is this for? The audience shapes the register, the level of complexity, and the assumptions the work can make.

What kind of thinking does this require? Analysis, argument, synthesis, description, evaluation. Naming the cognitive demand makes it visible before it is outsourced.

These three questions do not need to produce polished answers. They need to produce honest ones. A student who writes I am not sure what I am being asked to demonstrate has already done more useful work than a student who copies the task and opens the AI.

From those answers, students construct a prompt. Not a question. A specification that names the task, the audience, the thinking required, and any constraints that apply. Model this process explicitly the first time. Show students what a vague prompt returns versus a specified one. The contrast does more teaching than any explanation.

#### During the task

When you can see that a student has gone straight to copy-paste, the move that works is not to tell them what they did wrong. It is to make the gap visible to them through a question.

The redirecting question: Tell me what you were actually trying to show in this task. Not what the AI said. What were you trying to show. A student who has offloaded their thinking cannot answer this from their own thinking. That moment of not being able to answer is the intervention. It opens the conversation without accusation.

The narration move: Ask the student to walk you through what they did. Not what the output is but what they did to get there. Students who have genuinely engaged can narrate their process. Students who have not cannot narrate a process they did not do. The narration makes the cognitive work visible, or its absence visible, without requiring the teacher to make a judgement call first.

The pivot prompt: When a student has a piece of AI output in front of them and it is not serving the task, the most effective move is not to ask them to start again. Ask them to interrogate what they have. What is this missing for your specific task? What would someone who disagreed with this say? What does this not know about your context? This uses the existing output as raw material rather than discarding it, which reduces the resistance students feel to reconsidering their work.

> **The principle behind all three moves**
>
> None of these moves require the teacher to know more about AI than the student. They require the teacher to know what good thinking looks like. That is already what you do.

#### After the task

The V step is not complete when the work is submitted. The most useful after-task move is a brief process reflection that makes the V thinking visible and assessable.

Ask students to write two or three sentences answering: what did you specify in your initial prompt, and what would you change about that specification now that you have seen where the work went? This is not a punishment exercise for students who made mistakes. It is a reflection that applies to everyone, including students who validated well. The student who specified clearly and got useful output has something to say about why that worked. The student who did not has something to say about what they would do differently.

Over time, these reflections become the evidence of developing V thinking. They are also useful data for the teacher about where the V step is breaking down across a class.

### How V connects to the rest of the framework

V is the foundation. Every subsequent step in VERIFY depends on the student having a clear sense of what they were trying to do. A student who has not validated their task cannot meaningfully examine a source, because they have no basis for judging whether the source serves their purpose. They cannot reflect on whether the output fits their context, because they have not defined their context. They cannot filter for quality, because they have not established what quality would look like for this task.

This does not mean V has to be done perfectly before the student proceeds. It means that when something goes wrong later in the framework, it is worth checking whether the problem started at V. Most of the time it did.

> **V in one sentence**
>
> The prompt is where the thinking starts. V is the step that makes sure the thinking has actually started before the prompt is written.

## E — Examine the Source or System

Once a student has a piece of AI output in front of them, the question is not whether it looks right. The question is whether they have any basis for judging it. E is the step that builds that basis. It asks students to look at where the output came from, what the system cannot know, and what they would need to follow up before trusting what they have been given.

### The mechanism

AI output is always fluent. It is structured, confident, and grammatically correct regardless of whether it is accurate. Students who have been taught to use surface features like coherence and confidence as proxies for quality have no reliable way to identify a problem. The output looks exactly like good work whether it is or not.

This produces three distinct failure modes, each worth naming separately because each requires a different teacher response.

#### Uncritical acceptance

The most visible failure mode. The student reads the output, decides it looks right, and uses it without question. This is not credulity. It is the rational behaviour of someone who has not been taught what questions to ask of a source. If nobody has explained that AI systems can produce confident, plausible, wrong information, the student has no reason to expect it.

The teacher move here is not to explain that AI can be wrong, which students know abstractly, but to show them a specific instance of AI output that is wrong in a way that looks exactly like correct output. One concrete example does more than any general warning. Once a student has seen fluent, structured, convincingly wrong output, the question what would I need to check to know if this is right becomes a real question rather than a rhetorical one.

#### Bias blindness

The less visible and more durable failure mode. AI systems produce output in a generic, authoritative voice that carries no markers of perspective. Human sources carry obvious signals: an author's name, a publication, a date, a disciplinary frame. AI output carries none of those. Students who have been taught to ask whose view is this when reading a journal article or a newspaper do not automatically apply that question to AI output because nothing in the surface of the text signals that a perspective is present.

The perspective is present. AI training data reflects the contexts, assumptions, and framings of its sources. Output that discusses historical events, social issues, or professional practice will centre some perspectives and marginalise others, often invisibly. A student who does not know to look will not look.

> **Research note**
>
> The research identifies bias blindness as structurally compounded by AI: where a biased human source at least carries surface markers of authorship and context, AI output presents as neutral and authoritative by default. Students who have not been explicitly taught to interrogate apparent neutrality are at higher risk of accepting partial perspectives as complete ones.[^1]

The teacher move here is to make perspective visible as a concept before students interact with AI output. Not as a warning about AI specifically, but as a reading habit that applies to every source. AI output is a source. It has a perspective. The habit of asking whose view is centred here belongs in E, but it was built before E. If it has not been built, E is where the teacher names its absence.

#### Failure to backtrack or pivot

The failure mode teachers see most in extended research tasks. The student follows a line of AI output that does not serve the task, adds more prompts trying to fix it downstream, and arrives at a final piece that is off-target in a direction that was set at the beginning. They never stopped, recognised the trail was wrong, and went back to try a different approach.

This is a research skill, not an AI skill. The ability to recognise a dead end, backtrack to where the trail diverged, and try a different direction is fundamental to how research works. It is also a skill that is rarely taught explicitly any more. Students arrive at AI tools without a functional mental model of research as a process that involves wrong turns, because the instruction that would have built that model has largely been replaced by tasks that reward producing a finished product.

That gap is observable in student behaviour: arriving at a piece of AI output and treating it as the answer rather than as one possible direction. The question what would I do if this turned out to be wrong is one most students have never been asked.

> **What research as a process actually looks like — a reference for teachers to adapt**
>
> Most students have a product model of research: you look things up, you find answers, you write them down. Research is actually a trail model: you follow a question into a body of evidence, you assess what you find, you redirect when the trail goes cold, you build an understanding through accumulated decisions rather than a single lookup.
>
> The trail model has five moves students need to be able to do:
>
> Start with a question, not a topic. A topic produces a summary. A question produces an argument.
>
> Follow the trail. One source leads to another. The bibliography is not decoration; it is the next set of directions.
>
> Recognise a dead end. When a source does not serve the question, name that and stop. Do not build on a dead end.
>
> Backtrack to the point of divergence. Go back to where the trail split and take the other direction. This is not failure. It is how research works.
>
> Pivot when the question changes. Sometimes following a trail reveals that the original question was wrong or too narrow. Updating the question is progress, not drift.
>
> AI tools fit into the trail model at the start of a trail (orienting, scoping, generating directions to follow) and in the middle (checking understanding, identifying gaps). They are not reliable as the trail itself. A student who uses AI as the only trail has a product, not a research process.

### The classroom reality

The E failure that is easiest to miss is the student who has done something. They have not just copy-pasted. They have read the output, made some additions, adjusted the tone. The work looks considered. The problem is that the consideration was applied to the surface of the output rather than to its substance. They edited the words without interrogating the claims.

A reliable signal: ask a student to tell you, without looking at their work, what the three most important claims in it are and where each one comes from. A student who has genuinely examined the source can answer this. A student who has managed the output cannot. The answer reveals whether E happened or whether the student produced something polished and moved on.

The harder case is the student who examined the source partially. They checked one fact, found it was right, and used that confirmation to stop checking. Confirmation bias operates here the same way it operates with human sources: we look for evidence that what we already have is good and stop looking when we find it. The structure of AI output, which presents everything at the same level of confidence, makes this worse. There is no signal that some claims are better supported than others.

> **What E is not**
>
> E is not a fact-checking exercise. Asking students to verify every claim in an AI output is not scalable and misses the point. E is about building a reading stance: the habit of approaching any output with specific questions rather than general suspicion. A student with a good E habit does not check everything. They know what to check and why.

### Practitioner moves

The following moves are practitioner applications informed by the research on cognitive offloading, source evaluation, and the research skills gap. Adapt them to your subject and students.

#### Before the task

Before students use AI for research or information gathering, name the three failure modes explicitly. Not as warnings but as things to watch for. Tell students: here are the three ways people make mistakes with AI output, and here is what each one looks like in practice.

Then give them the research trail model. Even a brief version of the five moves above is more than most students have. Students who have a model for what research as a process is can apply it to AI. Students who only have a product model cannot.

If your school has a librarian, this is the moment to involve them. The research trail is their expertise. A fifteen-minute session with a librarian framed around how to follow a research trail using any source including AI is a concrete, useful investment before a research task begins.

#### During the task

The most effective during-task move for E is the source audit question. When a student has a piece of AI output and looks satisfied with it, ask: tell me the three most important claims in this piece and where each one comes from.

That question does three things at once. It forces the student to identify claims rather than just read text. It surfaces whether they have any idea where the claims originate. And it opens a conversation about how they would verify the ones they cannot account for, without requiring the teacher to accuse them of not checking.

The perspective question: Whose view is centred in this output? What context or experience would need to be present for this to be the complete picture? These questions apply to AI output the same way they apply to any source. Asking them routinely builds the habit.

The dead end question: Is this going in the right direction? If you found out tomorrow that one of the main claims here was wrong, would you still have a piece worth submitting? This question surfaces whether the student has any independent understanding of the territory, or whether everything depends on the AI output being right.

The backtrack invitation: When a student has gone down the wrong trail, do not redirect them to the correct answer. Ask them to identify where the trail went wrong and try the other direction. The experience of backtracking and finding a better path is the learning. The correct answer is not.

#### After the task

The most useful after-task move for E is a source reflection: ask students to identify, in two or three sentences, one claim in their work they are confident about and one they are less confident about, and explain the difference. This is not a gotcha. It is a metacognitive exercise that makes the student's relationship to their own sources visible and assessable.

Over time, the ability to distinguish between what you know and what you have accepted on trust is one of the most durable intellectual skills a student can develop. E is where that skill starts. The after-task reflection is where the teacher makes it visible enough to develop.

### How E connects to the rest of the framework

E depends on V having been done. A student who has not validated their task has no basis for judging whether a source serves it, because they have not defined what they need. The most common E failure, accepting output that does not fit the task, starts at V.

E feeds directly into R. A student who has genuinely examined the source arrives at R with real questions about fit and context. A student who has not arrives at R with a finished-looking piece they have already emotionally accepted. The metacognitive work of R is much harder when E has been skipped.

> **E in one sentence**
>
> Fluency is not accuracy. E is the step where students learn to treat those as separate questions.

## R — Reflect on Your Needs and Context

R is the hardest step in the framework to teach because it requires students to notice something about themselves: whether they have actually done any thinking yet, or whether they have received something that looks like thinking and accepted it. That kind of honest self-assessment does not come naturally to anyone. In a classroom, under time pressure, with a finished-looking piece of work already on the screen, it is genuinely difficult to ask students to pause and interrogate whether the work is really theirs.

It is also the step that makes the biggest difference to the quality of the final work. A student who applies R with honesty produces work that fits their specific task, reflects their specific context, and demonstrates their own thinking alongside whatever AI contributed. A student who skips it produces something generic that happens to be on the right topic.

### The mechanism

AI output is produced without knowledge of the student's specific context. It does not know what the teacher is actually assessing, what the student already understands, what the class has been studying, or what the student's own position on the question is. It produces a response that is generically competent for the topic. The student who accepts that response without asking whether it fits has outsourced not just the writing but the thinking that would have made the work specifically theirs.

The research describes this as the illusion of mastery: the output looks complete, so the student feels done. The feeling of completion is real. The learning that would have produced a genuine sense of completion has not happened. This is the gap R is designed to close.

> **Research note**
>
> The illusion of mastery is documented as one of the most consistent effects of unrestricted AI use in learning contexts. Students who outsource cognitive work report higher confidence in their understanding than students who complete the same task without AI assistance, while performing worse on subsequent assessments of that understanding. The confidence is produced by the quality of the output, not by the depth of the learning.[^5]

### Why the illusion is so difficult to puncture

The problem is not that students are incapable of honest self-assessment. The problem is that the AI output gives them no signal that self-assessment is needed. A first draft produced without AI is obviously incomplete: it has gaps, weak arguments, sentences that do not work. Those visible problems prompt revision. AI output has no visible problems. It is fluent, structured, and complete-looking at every stage. The normal signals that trigger reflection are absent.

This means R cannot rely on students noticing something is wrong. It has to be built as a deliberate step, a structured pause with specific questions, that happens regardless of how complete the work looks. The teacher's job at R is to make that pause happen and to make the questions specific enough that a student who has not yet done any real thinking cannot answer them convincingly.

### The classroom reality

The R failure teachers see most often is contextual mismatch: work that is technically competent but disconnected from the specific task. The argument is generic. The examples are not from the context the task specified. The level of complexity does not match the year level or the topic sequence. The student did not ask whether what they had was right for their situation. They asked whether it looked finished.

A subtler version: the student whose work is on target in terms of content but carries no trace of their own thinking. Every claim comes from the AI output. Every example is one the AI provided. When asked to talk about the work, the student can summarise it but cannot extend it, defend a claim they disagree with, or explain why they chose one argument over another. The work is accurate. The student is not inside it.

The most honest version of the R problem is this: the student has not yet made the work their own. R is the step where that happens, or does not.

> **The productive struggle principle**
>
> The cognitive effort of grappling with whether the work is really yours, what it is missing for your specific context, and what you actually think about the question is the effort that produces durable learning. R is the step that creates the conditions for that effort. A student who applies R honestly has done the hard work. The final product reflects that.

### Why you cannot make students struggle

The research is direct about this. You cannot compel productive struggle. You can only design conditions where avoiding the struggle costs more than doing it. Three approaches work in practice.

Make the process visible and assessable. If the reflection is assessed alongside the product, students who skip R pay a cost they can see. Annotated drafts, prompt histories, reflection logs, and process notes all make the thinking visible. A student who cannot produce evidence of their own thinking at R has a problem in the assessment, not just in their learning.

Name the discomfort directly. Tell students that R is uncomfortable on purpose. The feeling of sitting with a finished-looking piece and asking whether it is really yours is supposed to be uncomfortable. That discomfort is not a sign that something has gone wrong. It is the sign that the real work is about to start. Naming it removes the performance of certainty that causes students to move past R without doing it.

Give them a question they actually care about. The single most effective condition for genuine reflection is a question that matters to the student. When the content connects to something a student has a real position on, R happens more naturally. The teacher who chooses tasks that have genuine stakes for the students in the room makes every step of VERIFY easier. R most of all.

### Practitioner moves

The following moves are practitioner applications informed by the research on metacognition, productive struggle, and the illusion of mastery. Adapt them to your subject and students.

#### Before the task

Frame R before students start. Tell them explicitly: at a specific point in this task, you will stop and answer three questions honestly. Not for the teacher. For yourself. The questions are:

Does this fit my actual task? Not the topic. The specific task, the specific audience, the specific argument the teacher is asking you to make.

Does this reflect what I actually think? If you were asked to defend this in a conversation with no notes, could you? Is there a position here that is yours, or have you collected a position that belongs to the AI?

What is missing for my context? What does this output not know about your school, your subject, your class, your year level, your teacher's specific requirements?

These three questions have a useful property: a student who has genuinely engaged with V and E can answer them quickly because they have already done the thinking. A student who has not cannot answer them at all, which is the information the teacher needs.

#### During the task

The check-in is the primary during-task move for R. Not a formal check, a brief conversation at the point where students look like they are finishing. Two questions are enough.

Talk me through what you think about this topic. Not what the work says. What you think. A student who has done R can answer this with some confidence. A student who has not will describe the output rather than express a view. That distinction is immediate and clear.

What would you change if you were writing this for a different audience? This question reveals whether the student understands the specific context of their task or whether they have accepted a generic response. A student with genuine contextual understanding can name specific changes. A student without it cannot.

> **The teacher presence principle**
>
> R does not happen without a teacher who leans in. The check-in is not optional and it is not a bureaucratic step. It is the moment where the teacher finds out whether any learning has occurred. A teacher who skips the check-in because the work looks good has missed the point of R entirely.

#### After the task

The most useful after-task move for R is the ownership reflection. Ask students to write a short paragraph answering: what in this final piece of work is yours, and what did you take from AI and adapt? This is not a confession exercise. It is a thinking exercise. The student who can answer it clearly has done R. The student who cannot has told the teacher something important.

Over multiple tasks, these reflections become a record of developing thinking. They are also the most direct evidence available that a student is growing in their capacity to distinguish their own thinking from the thinking they have collected. That distinction is what the whole framework is building toward.

### How R connects to the rest of the framework

R is positioned at the centre of VERIFY because it is where the student has to bring themselves back into the work. V set the direction. E examined what the AI provided. R asks whether the student is present in the work at all.

A student who applies R honestly arrives at I in a genuinely curious state. They have identified what the work is missing for their context, which means they have real questions to investigate. A student who skips R arrives at I with nothing to look for. The investigation step is empty because the reflection step was skipped.

> **R in one sentence**
>
> The output looking finished is not the same as the thinking being done. R is the step that makes that distinction visible.

## I — Investigate Further

Every piece of AI output leaves something out. The question is whether the student knows what is missing, or whether they do not know because they have not looked. I is the step that makes looking deliberate. It asks students to treat what they have as a starting point rather than a destination, to ask what is not in this answer and to go find it.

I is also the most forgiving step in the framework. It works for students who have done V, E, and R thoroughly. It also works as an entry point for students who have not, because the questions it asks do not require prior context to be useful. A student who arrives at I with nothing done can still ask whose voice is missing here and start thinking. That question catches students regardless of where they started.

### The mechanism

Students who have done V, E, and R well arrive at I with genuine curiosity. They have a clear task, they have examined what the AI provided, they have reflected on what is missing for their context. The questions they bring to I are specific: what does this leave out about my topic, my community, my argument? They are investigating because they have identified a gap worth filling.

Students who have not done the earlier steps well arrive at I with a piece of AI output they have already emotionally committed to. Investigating further feels like questioning their own work, because the boundary between the AI output and their own thinking is unclear. These students need I most and find it most difficult. The teacher's job with this group is to make the investigation feel like addition rather than criticism.

### The dual function

I operates at two levels simultaneously and the teacher needs to hold both.

For students who have engaged genuinely with the earlier steps, I is about expanding and deepening. They are looking for what the AI structurally cannot provide: local knowledge, lived experience, perspectives that are underrepresented in training data, disciplinary nuance that requires a human expert rather than a statistical pattern. These students can reason about the limits of AI systems and use that reasoning to direct their investigation.

For students who have not yet engaged genuinely, I is the safety net. The question what is this leaving out, and who is not in this answer does not require built-up context to be useful. A student can ask it of any output at any point in the task and find something worth pursuing. The act of pursuing it often pulls them backwards through the framework: they start wondering about where the output came from, whether it fits their task, what they actually think about it. I becomes the entry point into the thinking that should have started at V.

> **The teacher use of this**
>
> When a student is stuck or has arrived at I with nothing to investigate, the question what is this leaving out is a reliable way in. It does not require the student to have done anything right before this moment. It just requires them to look at what they have and ask what is absent from it. Start there.

### The equity dimension

I is where the access and perspective gaps established in Part 1 become actionable for students. The two dimensions are distinct and both matter.

#### The access gap

Students in rural and remote communities, in under-resourced schools, and in communities not well represented in mainstream digital infrastructure are working with AI tools from a position of structural disadvantage. For these students, investigating further is not always straightforward: reliable access to databases, academic sources, and specialist materials may not be available in the same way as for students in well-resourced urban settings.

This does not mean I is less important for these students. It means the teacher needs to think carefully about what investigating further looks like in their specific context. Local knowledge, community expertise, and the student's own experience are legitimate sources of investigation. A student who supplements AI output with knowledge drawn from their own community is doing exactly what I asks: finding what the AI cannot provide.

> **Research note**
>
> Australian equity research documents that rural students face access barriers at rates significantly higher than national averages, with 76% of surveyed rural communities relying on mobile-only internet access. The implication for I is practical: investigating further looks different depending on the tools available, and the teacher's job is to make sure the investigation step is accessible to every student in the room, not just those with reliable broadband.[^7]

#### The perspective gap

AI training data reflects the contexts of its sources. For Australian students, this means systems that skew toward northern hemisphere settings, urban professional assumptions, and dominant cultural framings. Output on historical events, social issues, scientific debates, and cultural practices will centre some perspectives and marginalise others. A student who does not investigate further will not notice what is missing because the output does not signal its own gaps.

The most direct teacher move here is to make the question whose voice is not in this answer a standard part of I for every task. Not as a political exercise but as an information literacy one. Every source has a perspective. Every dataset has gaps. Every AI output reflects the limits of what it was trained on. Investigating further is how students find what the system cannot give them.

### The classroom reality

The I failure teachers see most is premature closure: the student decides the AI output is complete and does not look further. This is not a failure of curiosity. It is the absence of a mental model for what investigating further would even mean. If the output looks complete, there is nothing obvious to investigate.

The teacher move is to make incompleteness visible before it feels like a problem. Every piece of AI output is incomplete. That is not a criticism of the tool. It is a structural fact about any source. No source contains everything relevant to a question. The skill being built at I is the habit of asking what is not here rather than assuming that what is here is sufficient.

A more specific classroom reality: students who investigate further often find that their original direction was correct but needed specific local or contextual material to do the work the task required. They are not starting again. They are finishing the job. Framing I as completion rather than correction reduces the resistance students bring to it.

> **What investigating further is not**
>
> It is not fact-checking every claim. It is not producing a second AI output to compare with the first. It is not searching until you find a source that agrees with what you already have. It is asking what is genuinely missing from this picture and going to find it from a source that can provide it.

### Practitioner moves

The following moves are practitioner applications informed by the research on inquiry-based learning, equity in AI access, and the Chain of Learning framework. Adapt them to your subject and students.

#### Before the task

Prime the investigation before students start. Tell them that I is built into the task, that there will be a point where they are expected to find something the AI cannot provide. This changes the relationship students have with the AI output from the beginning. If they know they will need to go further, they are more likely to notice gaps as they work rather than treating the output as finished.

For tasks where the local or contextual dimension is significant, identify in advance what kinds of investigation are available to your students given their access and resources. Do not design an I step that requires database access if most students are working on mobile connections. Adjust the investigation expectation to match what genuine investigation looks like in your specific context.

#### During the task

The most effective during-task move for I is the gap question: what does this output not know about your specific situation, community, or argument? Ask it directly, to the whole class or to individual students, at the point where work looks like it might be wrapping up.

For students who have engaged with earlier steps: Ask what specific gap they identified at R and whether they have found anything to fill it. This connects I explicitly to R and makes the investigation purposeful rather than general.

For students who have not engaged with earlier steps: Use I as the entry point. Ask what is missing from this answer and follow the thread wherever it leads. If the student starts questioning the source, redirect to E. If they start questioning whether it fits their task, redirect to R. I can pull the whole framework into place retroactively.

> **The retroactive function**
>
> If a student has arrived at I without having done V, E, or R properly, do not send them back to the start. Use I to work backwards. What is this leaving out leads to E. Does this fit your task leads to V. Have you actually thought about this leads to R. The framework is a loop, not a line.

The AI as investigation tool: Students can use AI itself to investigate further. Prompts like what perspectives are not represented in this answer, what would a critic of this argument say, or what does this topic look like from a rural Australian context are legitimate investigation moves. The AI is not a reliable source of final answers, but it is a useful tool for generating directions to investigate from better sources.

#### After the task

The after-task move for I is an investigation log: a short account of what the student went looking for, where they looked, and what they found or did not find. This is not a bibliography. It is a record of the inquiry process. It makes the investigation visible, assessable, and useful as a reflection tool.

Students who found something significant at I and incorporated it into their work have a concrete example of the value of the step. Students who looked and found nothing useful have still done the work of identifying a gap and attempting to fill it. Both outcomes are worth recording.

### How I connects to the rest of the framework

I is the pivot point between examining what AI provides and making judgements about what to do with it. V set the direction. E examined the source. R asked whether the student was present in the work. I asks what is still missing and goes to find it.

A student who has genuinely applied I arrives at F with a richer, more specific body of material to evaluate. They have the AI output and the additional material they found. They have a basis for comparison. F asks them to filter that body of material for quality and fairness, which is a more tractable task when the material is specific rather than generic.

> **I in one sentence**
>
> Every source leaves something out. I is the step where students go looking for what that is.

## F — Filter for Quality and Fairness

Students cannot filter without a standard to filter against. Telling a student to check their work is only useful if they know what good work looks like. AI output removes the usual signals: it is always fluent, always structured, always confident. The markers students normally use to identify weak work are absent. F is the step that gives students a concrete set of questions to apply, so that filtering becomes an action rather than an instruction.

### The mechanism

The quality problem and the fairness problem are related but distinct and they need separate treatment in the classroom.

#### The quality problem

AI systems produce output at a consistent level of surface quality regardless of the accuracy of the underlying claims. A paragraph with three correct claims and one fabricated one looks identical to a paragraph with four correct claims. The fabricated claim is written with the same confidence, the same fluency, the same structural integration as the accurate ones. There is no visible seam.

Students who have been taught to use surface quality as a proxy for accuracy have no reliable tool for F. They read the output, it looks good, they accept it. The question they are not asking is whether the claims are accurate, evidenced, and specific enough for the task. F is where that question gets asked.

#### The fairness problem

Fairness is harder to teach than quality because it is easier to treat as abstract. A student can accept intellectually that AI output may not represent all perspectives without ever applying that understanding to a specific piece of work. Abstract fairness awareness produces nothing. Concrete questions produce action.

> **Research note**
>
> The research identifies fairness as a dimension of AI literacy that is rarely operationalised in classroom practice. Students who are told AI can be biased do not automatically develop the habit of checking for bias. Explicit check questions, applied routinely, are the mechanism that moves fairness from concept to practice.[^9]

The approach that works is to give students specific questions they ask of every output. Questions that are short enough to remember, concrete enough to answer, and direct enough that a student cannot give a performance answer without actually thinking. Those questions are below.

### The check questions

The following seven questions are the core of F. Students can ask them of themselves as a self-check, or put them directly to the AI as follow-up prompts to generate better output. Both uses are legitimate. Both build the same habit.

The questions are divided into quality and fairness, but in practice they overlap. A quality problem is often a fairness problem in disguise: an output that cannot be sourced is frequently one that reflects a partial perspective presented as a complete one.

#### Quality

**1. Can I point to a source for the three most important claims in this output?**

If the answer is no, the student does not yet have the basis for the work. This is not a request to footnote everything. It is a test of whether the student has any independent grip on the content.

**2. Does this answer the question I actually asked, or an easier version of it?**

AI systems simplify. They produce the most generically competent response for the topic rather than the most specific response for the task. This question surfaces that gap.

**3. What would someone who disagreed with this say, and does this output mention that?**

The absence of counterargument is a quality signal and a fairness signal simultaneously. An output that presents one position as uncontested is incomplete regardless of whether that position is correct.

#### Fairness

**4. Whose experience is this output written from?**

Not a rhetorical question. A real one. The student should be able to name a context, a perspective, a set of assumptions that are present in the output. If they cannot, the output has presented itself as neutral and the student has accepted that.

**5. Who is missing from this answer, and does that matter?**

The follow-on from question 4. Identifying whose experience is present makes it possible to identify whose is absent. Whether the absence matters depends on the task. The student decides that. The question just makes the decision conscious.

**6. Is this written for my specific situation, or for a generic one that may not be mine?**

Particularly relevant for Australian students working with systems trained predominantly on northern hemisphere content. The output may be accurate in general and wrong for the specific context.

#### The combined question

**7. If I took out everything I cannot personally verify or add to, what would be left?**

The hardest question on the list. It cannot be answered with a performance. Either something is left when the unverifiable material is removed, or there is not. The student knows which one is true. Use this question as a private reflection rather than a public one. It is most useful when the student asks it of themselves honestly.

> **How to introduce the questions**
>
> Do not hand students a list of seven questions and ask them to work through it. Introduce one or two questions per task until the habit builds. Start with question 1 and question 5, because they are the most concrete and produce the most immediate, visible action. Add the others as the class develops confidence with F. Question 7 is for when the earlier ones are embedded.

### The classroom reality

The F failure teachers see most is surface compliance: the student reads the check questions, decides their output passes, and moves on without actually checking anything. This is not dishonesty. It is the absence of a checking habit. A student who has never been taught to interrogate their own work will interpret do you think this is fair as a yes/no question with an obvious right answer.

The move that breaks surface compliance is to ask students to show their work on a specific question. Not do you think this is accurate, but tell me the source for the claim in paragraph two. Not do you think this is fair, but whose experience is this written from. The specific question requires a specific answer. The specific answer reveals whether the checking happened.

A reliable classroom signal that F has been done properly: the student has changed something in their work as a result of the check. Not necessarily a major revision. A specific addition, a qualification, an acknowledgement of a missing perspective. F that produces no changes is F that was performed rather than applied.

### Practitioner moves

The following moves are practitioner applications informed by the research on evaluative thinking, fairness in AI systems, and the development of self-regulated learning habits.

#### Before the task

Before students use the check questions for the first time, show them what a failed F looks like. Take a piece of AI output, real or constructed, and walk through it using questions 1, 3, and 5. Show the class what it looks like when a claim cannot be sourced, when counterargument is absent, and when a perspective is missing. Name what you are doing and why.

This is not a warning about AI. It is a demonstration of what careful reading looks like. The check questions are the tool. The demonstration is what makes them usable.

#### During the task

The most effective during-task move for F is to ask students to apply one specific question to their current output and tell you what they found. Not all seven. One.

Question 1 as a spot check: Ask a student to tell you the source for one specific claim in their work. The answer tells you immediately whether F has started or not.

Question 5 as a class discussion: Put a piece of AI output on the screen and ask the class collectively who is missing from this answer. The discussion is the learning. The students who cannot identify anyone missing have told you something about where their F habit is.

Question 7 as a private prompt: Ask students to answer question 7 in writing, for themselves only. Not to submit. Not to share. Just to know. A student who does this honestly and finds almost nothing left has the information they need to understand why F matters.

#### After the task

The most useful after-task move for F is a brief filter log: one sentence on what the student changed as a result of applying the check questions, and why. If they changed nothing, they write that too, and say why they were satisfied. Both answers are legitimate. Both require the student to have actually applied F rather than performed it.

Over time, the filter logs become evidence of a developing evaluative habit. A student whose logs show increasingly specific changes is developing F. A student whose logs consistently say I changed nothing has told the teacher something important about where the teaching needs to go next.

> **The habit builds over time**
>
> The first time students use the check questions they will be slow and uncertain. The fifth time they will be faster. The twentieth time some of the questions will be running automatically before the student formally applies F. That automatic running is the goal. The check questions are a scaffold, not a permanent structure.

### How F connects to the rest of the framework

F is the step where the accumulated work of V, E, R, and I gets evaluated against a standard. The student has defined their task, examined the source, reflected on fit, and investigated what was missing. F asks whether what they now have is good enough and fair enough to stand behind.

A student who has done the earlier steps well arrives at F with a richer body of material and a clearer sense of what the task requires. The filtering is more tractable because the earlier work has already removed some of the problems. F is where the final standard is applied.

F leads directly into Y, where the student has to stand behind their judgement. A student who has applied F honestly arrives at Y knowing what they checked, what they found, and what they decided. That is the basis for genuine intellectual ownership.

> **F in one sentence**
>
> Students cannot filter without a standard. The check questions are the standard made concrete.

## Y — Your Own Judgement

Y is where the framework pays off or reveals that it did not. A student who has worked through V, E, R, I, and F with genuine engagement arrives at Y with a position. They can explain what they decided and why. They can say what they considered and rejected. They can defend a claim without looking at their notes because the thinking is theirs, not borrowed.

A student who has performed the earlier steps without engaging with them arrives at Y with nothing to stand behind. The work is finished. The thinking has not happened. Y makes that visible, which is why it is the most valuable diagnostic in the framework and the most uncomfortable step for students who have not done the work.

### The mechanism

You cannot defend a judgement you did not make. This is the core of Y and it is not a metaphor. When a student is asked to explain their reasoning and the reasoning came from an AI output they accepted without interrogation, they have no access to it. They can describe the output. They cannot explain the reasoning because the reasoning was never theirs.

This produces a specific and recognisable classroom moment: the student who can summarise their work but cannot extend it. Ask them what they think about the topic, separate from what they wrote, and the answer is a restatement of the output. Ask them what they would change if the task required a different argument and they have no answer. The work is done. The understanding is not there.

> **Research note**
>
> The research on self-regulated learning identifies the capacity to evaluate and defend one's own reasoning as a marker of genuine understanding rather than surface performance. Students who can produce correct answers without being able to explain their reasoning have acquired output, not knowledge. Y is the step that distinguishes between the two.[^11]

### Y as diagnostic

Y works as the final step in the framework and as a diagnostic that runs backwards through the whole cycle. A student who cannot apply Y genuinely is showing the teacher exactly where the process broke down. The teacher's job is to read that signal accurately and respond to it.

Cannot explain the reasoning: the problem started at V. The student did not validate the task clearly enough to understand what kind of thinking it required.

Can explain but cannot defend against challenge: the problem started at E. The student accepted the output without interrogating its claims or sources.

Can defend the argument but it does not fit the task: the problem started at R. The student reflected on the output in general terms but not on whether it served their specific context.

Has a position but it is entirely generic: the problem started at I. The student did not investigate what the output was missing and so arrived at Y with nothing specific to stand behind.

Has specific material but cannot evaluate it: the problem started at F. The student collected material but did not apply a standard to it.

### What Y is not

Y is not a confidence exercise. The goal is not to make students feel good about their work. The goal is for students to know what they think and why, and to have work that reflects that. A student who applies Y honestly and finds they are not confident has found something true and useful. That is a better outcome than a student who performs confidence they do not have.

Y is not a final polish step. It is not the moment for proofreading or formatting. It is the moment for intellectual ownership. If a student arrives at Y and starts fixing commas, they have misunderstood the step.

Y is not only for students who have done everything right. A student who engaged partially with the earlier steps can still apply Y to whatever they have. The question what do I actually think about this is available to any student at any point. Y just makes it the final and non-negotiable question before the work is submitted.

> **The honest version of Y**
>
> A student who arrives at Y and realises they have nothing to stand behind has learned something more valuable than a student who arrives at Y with a polished piece they cannot explain. The realisation is the learning. The teacher who creates space for that realisation without penalising it is doing the most important work in the framework.

### The classroom reality

The Y failure that is hardest to address is the student who believes the work is theirs. They spent time on it. They made decisions about it. They edited it. The fact that the thinking underneath it came from an AI output they accepted without interrogation is not visible to them because the line between their contribution and the AI's was never drawn.

The teacher move here is not to challenge the ownership claim but to test it. Ask the student to talk about the topic for two minutes without looking at their work. Ask them to tell you what the three most interesting things they found out while doing this task were. Ask them what they would do differently if they had to start again. A student who genuinely owns the work can answer these questions. A student who does not will find them harder than expected.

The harder case is the student who knows the work is not really theirs and has decided that is acceptable. This is not a Y problem. It is an integrity and assessment design problem. VERIFY does not solve academic integrity issues on its own. What it does is make genuine intellectual ownership visible enough that its absence is also visible. The assessment response to that is the teacher's call.

### Practitioner moves

The following moves are practitioner applications informed by the research on intellectual ownership, self-regulated learning, and assessment design.

#### Before the task

Name Y at the start. Tell students that the final question for every task is: can you explain what you decided and why, in your own words, without looking at your work. Not as a threat. As the definition of what the task is trying to produce. The goal is not a piece of work. The goal is a student who has thought about something carefully enough to have a position on it.

When students know from the beginning that Y is coming, they make different decisions earlier in the process. The student who knows they will need to explain their reasoning is more likely to track their reasoning as they work. Naming Y in advance is one of the most effective ways to make V, E, R, I, and F happen more genuinely.

#### During the task

The during-task move for Y is the early ownership check: a brief conversation at the midpoint of the task where the teacher asks the student to say, in a sentence or two, what they think about the question. Not what they have found. What they think.

If the student has a position: ask them where it came from and whether their work reflects it. This connects Y back through the earlier steps and makes the ownership visible before the task is finished, when there is still time to act on it.

If the student does not yet have a position: that is useful information at midpoint. Ask them what they would need to know or do before they could form one. That question sends them back into the framework with a specific purpose.

If the student gives back the AI output as their position: ask them whether they agree with it. A simple question. The student who says yes and can say why has started forming a genuine position. The student who says yes and cannot say why has told the teacher something important.

> **The two-minute test**
>
> The most reliable during-task move for Y is to ask any student to talk about their topic for two minutes without looking at anything. What they can say fluently is what they know. What they struggle to say is where the thinking has not happened yet. Two minutes is enough to distinguish between the two.

#### After the task

The most useful after-task move for Y is a short defence: three to five minutes where the student explains their work verbally to the teacher or to a peer. Not a presentation. A conversation. The teacher asks two or three questions. The student answers from their own thinking.

This does not have to be formally assessed, though it can be. Its primary value is as a feedback loop. The student who struggles to defend their work at Y has immediate, specific feedback about where their engagement with the framework broke down. The teacher has the same information. Both can act on it before the next task.

Over multiple tasks, the verbal defence becomes easier. Students who know it is coming prepare differently. Students who do it regularly develop the habit of tracking their own reasoning as they work. That habit is the long-term goal of the whole framework.

### Y and intellectual ownership

The goal the whole framework is building toward is a student who has taken information, interacted with it, and found ways to make it interesting to them rather than just completing a task. A student who arrives at Y with that relationship to their work does not need to be asked whether the work is theirs. It is visible in how they talk about it.

That student does not need AI to have been absent from their process. AI can be part of how they gathered information, tested ideas, and drafted arguments. What matters is that the student was also present in the process: making decisions, forming positions, interrogating what the AI provided, and arriving at a piece of work they can stand behind because they built it rather than received it.

That is what VERIFY is for. Not to remove AI from the learning process. To make sure the student is in it.

> **Y in one sentence**
>
> You cannot defend a judgement you did not make. Y is the step where the student finds out whether they made one.
